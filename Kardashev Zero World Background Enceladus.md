# Kardashev Zero World Background: Enceladus

Oct 20, 2026 · @Jeff Pyles

## Overview

Enceladus is the game's dark-ocean world: a 504 km ice moon of Saturn whose species evolves in a sealed, lightless ocean with no fire, about 1% of Earth's gravity, and a tidal pulse every 33 hours.

- **Technology without fire:** the tech path runs through stone, glass, grown minerals, electrochemistry and bred organisms, with fire arriving only after the surface is reached.
- **A world perceived by sound and chemistry:** the map starts dark and is revealed by sonar, chemical trails and electric fields, not sight.
- **The ice is the first frontier:** breaking through 5 to 35 km of ice is this species' equivalent of reaching space. Once on the surface, actual orbit is easy.
- **A built-in direction:** Saturn's magnetic field points toward the south pole everywhere in the ocean, toward the one place where the world opens.

Physical figures here are approximate, drawn from Cassini (2004 to 2017) and later studies. Everything about life and culture is speculative design, chosen to be plausible within those physics.

## Physical structure and surface

Enceladus is small, bright, cold and tidally locked, with old cratered ground in the north and a young, active south pole that sprays its ocean into space.

| Property | Value (approximate) |
| --- | --- |
| Diameter | 504 km |
| Distance from Saturn | 238,000 km |
| Orbit and day | 32.9 hours (tidally locked) |
| Surface gravity | 0.113 m/s², about 1.2% of Earth's |
| Escape velocity | about 240 m/s |
| Typical surface temperature | about −200 °C |
| Warmest spots on the tiger stripes | about −80 °C or colder |
| Sunlight | about 1% of Earth's |

**Terrain.** Three kinds of ground: heavily cratered old plains in the north (such as the Ali Baba and Aladdin craters), belts of grooves and ridges where the crust was stretched (Samarkand Sulci), and smooth resurfaced plains (Sarandib Planitia). The south polar terrain, bounded by scarps near 55° south, is nearly crater-free and among the youngest ground in the Solar System. Features are named after the Arabian Nights.

**Tiger stripes.** Four parallel fractures near the south pole, each about 130 km long and roughly 35 km apart: Alexandria, Cairo, Baghdad and Damascus Sulci. Each is a trough flanked by ridges, warmer than the surrounding ice, and the source of the plumes.

**Plumes.** Discovered by Cassini in 2005. Roughly 100 jets along the stripes release on the order of 200 kg of water vapor per second, carrying ice grains with salt, silica and organic molecules.

- Most grains fall back as deep, fine frost over the south polar region, in places possibly hundreds of meters thick.
- The rest escapes and feeds Saturn's faint E ring. In 2023 the James Webb Space Telescope saw a vapor plume stretching about 10,000 km.
- The plumes are brightest when Enceladus is farthest from Saturn and dimmest near its closest point.

**The sky from the surface.** On the Saturn-facing side, Saturn hangs motionless about 28° across, roughly 55 times the full Moon's width, going through phases every 33 hours. Its rings appear edge-on as a thin bright line, because Enceladus orbits in the ring plane. The Sun is a tiny point, still thousands of times brighter than a full Moon. The sky is black at noon.

## Interior: ice, ocean, rock

Under the ice is a global ocean about 30 km deep, resting on a porous rocky core that tides keep warm, with hot water reacting with rock at the seafloor.

&#91;embedded content: cross-section · ice, ocean, core\]

If surface oxidants do reach the ocean, the water between ceiling and floor becomes a chemical battery that life could tap.

- **Ice shell:** about 20 to 25 km thick on average, thickest at the equator (around 30 to 35 km), and possibly under 5 km at the south pole. It floats freely on the ocean, revealed in 2015 by a slight wobble in the moon's rotation.
- **Ocean:** roughly 26 to 31 km deep, all the way around, a little below 0 °C and salty. Seafloor pressure is only about what you'd feel 500 m down in Earth's ocean, because gravity is so weak.
- **Core:** about 380 km across, likely loose, porous rock that seawater circulates through. Tidal flexing heats it, and friction in the core may be the main heat source. Too small to have melted, it probably never formed an iron core.

**What the plumes tell us about the ocean:**

- Salts, mainly sodium chloride and carbonates, pointing to water in contact with rock. Models suggest it is alkaline, around pH 8.5 to 11.
- Tiny silica grains that likely form only in water hotter than about 90 °C: evidence of hydrothermal vents.
- Molecular hydrogen, a chemical energy source that some Earth microbes live on.
- Organic molecules, including large complex ones, and hydrogen cyanide, a starting material for amino acids and nucleotides.
- Phosphates (reported 2023) at concentrations far above Earth's oceans.

**The core as a resource base.** Nobody has sampled it, but rock soaking in warm alkaline water should yield serpentine and talc, fibrous serpentine (chrysotile), chert and opal, carbonates and brucite, clays, magnetite, awaruite (a natural nickel-iron alloy), iron and nickel sulfides such as pyrite, graphite and phosphates. How each is used is under Material culture.

**Biggest unknown: age.** Estimates for the ocean's age run from tens of millions to billions of years, which matters enormously for whether life could start. A good per-seed variable.

## Forces from Saturn

Saturn shapes the ocean through tides, which supply heat and a 33-hour rhythm, and through its magnetic field, which gives a steady southward direction. Almost no other signal from outside reaches the water.

**Tides.** Enceladus's orbit is slightly oval, kept that way by a 2:1 orbital resonance with the moon Dione. Saturn's pull stretches the moon more when close and less when far, and the bulge rocks slightly side to side.

- The changing part of the pull is only about 0.02% of the moon's gravity, too small to feel directly.
- Its effects are large: the shell flexes, water sloshes, currents reverse, vents may pulse, and the core is heated by friction.
- Eruptions peak about five hours after peak tidal stress.

**The tiger stripe cracks.** In the leading model each stripe is a water-filled slot about a meter wide running from the ocean to the surface. Narrower or wider slots can't sustain themselves.

- Slots flex rather than slam: our rough estimate is a swing of tens of centimeters per orbit. They never fully close.
- As a slot widens, its water level drops and ocean water is drawn in; as it narrows, water is flushed back down.
- Because ice floats, the water level sits roughly a tenth of the way down through the shell, perhaps a few hundred meters below the surface at the south pole. Above it, water boils into a vapor jet that turns supersonic near the vents.

**Magnetic field.** Saturn's field at Enceladus is about 100 times weaker than Earth's, but a steady field passes through ice and water unchanged.

- It is aligned almost exactly with Saturn's spin axis, and Enceladus orbits in Saturn's equatorial plane, so the field runs along the moon's own axis, pointing toward its south pole.
- It barely changes, so it gives direction but no sense of time.
- Just above the south pole, the field bends around the plume. Cassini's magnetometer noticed this in 2005, which led to the plumes' discovery.

**Light and radio.** Visible light dies within tens of meters of ice; infrared, ultraviolet and X-rays stop sooner; gamma rays penetrate only meters. Radio passes through kilometers of cold, pure ice, so radar from orbit could detect the ocean beneath the thin south polar shell, though salty water reflects radio rather than letting it in. No sunlight ever reaches the ocean.

## Senses and signals

In a lightless ocean, the useful senses are chemistry, sound, flow, electric fields and magnetism, with tides as the clock.

| Sense | What it detects | What it gives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chemistry | Trails of hydrogen, salt and warmth from vents | The main landscape; navigation to food |
| Sound | Ice cracking and groaning on the tidal rhythm, vents hissing, calls | Long-range sensing, echolocation, communication |
| Flow | Tidal currents and circulation shaped by the 33-hour spin | Direction and rhythm |
| Electric fields | Weak natural currents at vents, carried well by salty water | Close-range hunting and mapping, as in sharks |
| Magnetism | Saturn's steady field, pointing south | A compass, never a clock |
| Gravity | A weak but steady pull | A faint sense of up and down between ceiling and floor |
| Light | Only what living things make; vents are likely too cool to glow | Signals and lures, once bioluminescence evolves |

**Tides as the clock.** The tidal pulse is felt through its side effects: the flexing shell, reversing currents, pulsing vents, and the south polar cracks opening and closing. Earth's tidal animals keep time the same way, by water level and flow.

**Magnetic sense.** Some Earth bacteria steer by chains of magnetite crystals, and they can tell north-seeking from south-seeking. In a field 100 times weaker, organisms would need far more magnetite, but it's plausible. A south-seeking sense would point every creature, everywhere, toward the opening at the pole.

**Sound channel.** If temperature and salt layering form a sound channel like Earth's ocean has, calls could carry across much of the moon, as whale song does here.

## Speculative evolution

Life likely starts as microbes in the warm rock of the core and climbs, slowly and sparsely, toward long-lived, boneless minds that think in the cold and talk in song. Energy is the constant limit at every stage.

1. **Chemists in the rock.** Microbes in the core's water-filled pores combine hydrogen made by water reacting with rock with dissolved carbon dioxide, producing methane and a little energy. Lab experiments show some Earth microbes of this kind can grow under simulated Enceladus conditions. The core, a rock sponge 380 km across, may be the largest habitat; the open ocean is the frontier.
2. **Glass reefs and oases.** Vents are rich but short-lived, sometimes lasting decades. Two strategies win:
   - Partnership: hosts with no gut that farm microbes, like Earth's vent tubeworms.
   - Dispersal: hardy spores that drift dormant for years until they reach a fresh vent.

   Silica is plentiful, so colonies build glass lattices, like Earth's glass sponges in cold deep water. In 1% gravity with gentle currents, these could grow into branching towers kilometers tall.
3. **The ocean battery.** Large animals on Earth appeared only after oxygen made metabolism far more efficient. Here, Saturn's radiation turns surface ice into oxidants such as oxygen and peroxide, and some may be cycled down into the ocean at the active south pole. Fuel rises from the floor, oxidant seeps from the ceiling, and whatever bridges the gap wins. The defining behavior may be vertical migration of tens of kilometers between ice and vents, timed to the tides.
4. **Drifters and hunters.** Between oases lies vast, cold, dark water, favoring slow, efficient, long-range swimmers.
   - Drifters relax into radial symmetry, since weak gravity barely separates up from down: translucent glass-bell forms trailing filaments.
   - Hunters go streamlined and bilateral, hunting by echolocation and electric sense, perhaps with glowing lures.
   - Predator-prey arms races drive nervous systems toward complexity, as in Earth's Cambrian explosion. Eyes pay only once something makes light, and then glowing and seeing can escalate together.
5. **Slow minds.** Cold slows everything, so intelligence is likely slow and long-lived; Greenland sharks in Arctic water live around 400 years.
   - The closest Earth model for the body is the octopus: no skeleton, flexible arms that manipulate and taste, much of the nervous system in the arms.
   - The pressure toward intelligence is knowing where vents are and predicting when they will die, a problem passed across generations, so cultural memory carried in song is valuable.
6. **Through the ceiling.** The species reaches the surface (see Reaching the surface).

## Social culture

Enceladans are imagined as largely solitary thinkers bound together by long-range song, so their great early challenge is cooperation, and their myths center on the south pole.

**Starting profile.** Real octopuses are mostly solitary, but big brains tend to evolve in social species. Enceladans sit in between.

- Strengths: long lives, strong individual learning, deep experience per individual.
- Weaknesses: cooperation is hard and knowledge transfer depends on song rather than daily contact, so specialization and division of labor come late.
- The first institution that gets solitary thinkers to cooperate is their agricultural revolution.

**Song as memory.** Vents live and die over decades, so knowing which are alive is life-or-death knowledge. It would be carried in songs that encode routes and vent histories across generations, much as Aboriginal songlines encode navigation across vast distances.

**The compass as creed.** Every creature with a magnetic sense feels the same pull south, toward the one place where the world opens. That would likely become a founding belief: a sense given from birth that there is somewhere to go, and a driving force for expansion.

**Cosmology.** Their world is a closed sphere of water with a ceiling. Travel far enough in one direction and you return to where you started. There are no stars. At the south pole, water rushes upward and never returns, so that would sit at the center of their myths and eventually their science.

**Science reaching outward.** The 33-hour tide, its strength, and slow swings in heating as the orbit changes over millennia all imply a massive unseen body and others tugging on it. Their Newton could deduce Saturn, and even its other moons, before anyone breaks through the ice to look.

**On the surface, silence.** Sound, smell and electric sense don't carry through vacuum. A people whose world was made of song would step into total silence and might first communicate by tapping vibrations through the ice.

## Material culture

With no fire and no free oxygen underwater, Enceladan technology is cold-formed: knapped stone, grown minerals, glass fiber, electroplated metal and bred organisms, closer to farming and glassblowing than to metalwork.

| Material | Source | Uses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chert and opal (silica) | Deposited by vent water | Knapped cutting edges, the first tools, as flint was for humans |
| Glass fiber | Guided silica growth by bred organisms | Lattice structures, cord, composites |
| Serpentine and talc | Water-altered core rock | Carving stone; talc as a lubricant |
| Chrysotile | Fibrous serpentine | Mineral cord, twisted alongside glass fiber |
| Carbonates and brucite | Alkaline vents; microbes that deposit carbonate | Grown stone walls and cement at 0 °C, like Earth's Lost City vent chimneys (about 60 m tall) |
| Clays | Altered rock | Unfired seals and packing |
| Magnetite | Byproduct of water reacting with rock | Lodestone, significant to a species with a magnetic sense |
| Awaruite | Native nickel-iron alloy grains | Separated magnetically; feedstock for metal work |
| Iron and nickel sulfides (pyrite) | Core rock | Conductors; pyrite crystals were early radio detectors on Earth |
| Graphite | Core rock | Conductors |
| Phosphates | Ocean and rock | Fertilizer, for anything they farm |

**Metal without fire.**

- **Electrochemistry:** electroplating works at 0 °C. Metals dissolved in vent fluids, or leached from sulfides, can be deposited as solid metal by passing a current through the solution. Vents are natural batteries, and this is a people who sense electric fields, so electrodeposition could be their first metallurgy: slow, cold and precise.
- **Biomineralization:** chitons grow teeth capped with magnetite, among the hardest materials any organism makes, and limpet teeth reinforced with iron-oxide fibers are among the strongest biological materials known. Bred organisms could be living foundries for hard edges.

**The limit.** Without heat they can't fuse awaruite grains or build large, tough metal structures. That changes only at the surface, where oxidants in the ice make a first sealed fire possible.

## Reaching the surface

The route to the surface is up the tiger stripes: ride the tidal inflow up the water-filled slot, then climb the last few hundred meters against a freezing vapor gale, anchored by a line, in an inflated insulating suit.

**The passage.**

- Travelers ride the inflow up as the slot widens, and the flush back down if they need to retreat.
- At the water level, water boils because the pressure is near zero, not because it's hot. Boiling chills it, so the surface is freezing, boiling slush.
- Above it, the vapor accelerates to supersonic speed. By our rough estimate, it pushes on a body about as hard as a gale-force wind on Earth, a few hundred newtons on something person-sized, far more than its weight in 1% gravity. Without an anchor, climbers are blown out.
- Hazards for anchor lines: frost coats everything in the channel, slots breathe and may slide sideways each orbit, and the vents themselves clog and shift.

**Suits.** At 0 °C, water stays liquid above about 600 pascals, so a suit holding a few thousand pascals (a few percent of Earth's air pressure) keeps a water-filled body from boiling. That's a gently inflated membrane, not a rigid vessel. Glass fiber wound into tough organic resin is essentially fiberglass, how we build pressure tanks. Vacuum insulates; the real danger is touching ice at −200 °C, so suits need insulated contact points or legs.

**A domed harbor.** Capping a section of crack stops the gale: vapor fills the dome until it reaches about 600 pascals and the flow stops.

- The dome must stay at or above the water's temperature, or it becomes a frost trap and fills with ice. Condensing vapor releases heat; contact with the ice is the main drain, so floors and footings need insulation.
- It must be anchored, not weighted: in this gravity, 1,000 pascals of pressure equals the weight of almost 9 tonnes per square meter. Fiber anchors frozen into ice as strong as rock would hold it.
- Pressurizing the dome pushes the water level down, nearly 9 m for every 1% of Earth's air pressure. Options: a dry vapor shaft with a hoist (lifting 500 m costs what 6 m does on Earth), or a flooded shaft sealed by a lock at the natural water level, giving a pool under the dome where water-breathers can swim up from the ocean.
- Natural slots maintain themselves at about a meter wide. Enlarging one means supplying heat indefinitely; melting a 5 m shaft through 5 km of ice takes roughly 13 gigawatt-hours by our rough estimate. A water-filled shaft resists inward creep; a dry one is stable only in the cold, rigid ice near the top.
- The crack breathes and may shear, so linings must flex. Power could come from the tidal pumping itself, like a tidal mill, or from burning surface oxidants with hydrogen or methane carried up from below.

**What the surface brings.**

- Hazards: vacuum and −200 °C cold, radiation (far gentler than at Jupiter's moons but new to this biology), deep loose frost, cracks that open and close, and the loss of sound, smell and electric sense.
- Light: eyes tuned to bioluminescence would be overwhelmed even by 1% sunlight. They may first know light as something that warms or burns, and build instruments to translate it.
- Gifts: sunlight as a new energy source; the oxidants that fed their ocean from above, now at the source; a first fire in a sealed vessel; and perhaps iron meteorites lying in the ice. Meteoric iron was humanity's first iron too, and their first metal would fall from the sky they had just discovered.
- The view: the ceiling was never the edge of the universe. Overhead is the giant planet their Newton deduced from the tides.
- Orbit is then easy: escape takes only about 240 m/s, against about 8 km/s just to reach orbit from Earth.

## Game design implications and open questions

Enceladus plays as a vertical, dark, slow world whose milestones run from cooperation to the ceiling, and whose many unknowns make good per-seed variables.

**Implications.**

- **View:** a cross-section from rock floor to ice ceiling suits it, since vertical travel and migration are central.
- **Map:** dark by default, revealed by sound pulses, chemical trails and electric sensing, so perception is something players research and build.
- **Clock:** the 33-hour tide is the natural unit of game time, driving currents, vent pulses and the crack passage window.
- **Kardashev start:** a cold, slow metabolism puts Enceladans much further below K = 0 than humans, so milestones may need to be relative to their start.
- **Neighbors:** Titan also orbits Saturn, so Enceladans and Titans would likely meet long before anyone from Earth arrives.

**Possible milestones.**

1. First cooperative institution among solitary thinkers
2. First knapped tool and first grown structure
3. First electroplated metal
4. First creature to reach the ice ceiling
5. First foothold on the surface, and first sealed fire
6. A domed harbor over a tiger stripe
7. First object in orbit

**Open questions and seed variables.**

- [ ] Ocean age: tens of millions to billions of years
- [ ] Core mineral composition and vent temperatures
- [ ] Whether surface oxidants actually reach the ocean, and how much
- [ ] Crack width, how far slots flex each orbit, and sideways shear
- [ ] Whether a sound channel forms, and how far calls carry
- [ ] How social Enceladans are at the start, kept as adjustable traits for playtesting
